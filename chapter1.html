An operator in a control room can watch and switch equipment hundreds of kilometres away. Part 1 explains how: the control philosophy behind SCADA, the parts that make it up, the difference between an RTU and a PLC, and how data travels in both directions. Every section has something to try.

  • Watch: readings from far-away sites arrive on the control room screen every few seconds.
  • Control: an operator's click travels back out to open a breaker or start a pump.
  • Decide: the human stays in charge, helped by alarms and trends.

1.1Control philosophies

Every control system answers one question: who makes the decision, the machine or the human? There are three answers.

Machine decidesSharedHuman decides

A hybrid system at work: water treatment

Water treatment plants run by Air Selangor or SAINS are a good example. The idea is simple: the machine handles the routine, the human handles the unexpected.

The PLC does this by itself

  • Runs the intake pump that draws water from the river.
  • Adds chlorine to kill germs: more water in, more chlorine; less water, less chlorine. A fixed rule, so no human needed.

The operator decides this

  • After heavy rain the river turns muddy. The sensor sees cloudy water and the SCADA screen shows an alarm.
  • The PLC has no rule for this, so the operator chooses: stop the intake pump until the river clears, or backwash the filter (flush it clean).
Think of cruise control in a car. It keeps your speed steady without you touching the pedal: that is the automatic part. When a lorry suddenly brakes ahead, you take over, because cruise control cannot judge that: that is the supervisory part. Both together make a hybrid system, and SCADA works the same way.

1.2What is SCADA?

Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) is a system of software and hardware that lets industrial organisations control processes locally or at remote locations, and monitor, gather and process real-time data.

Tap each letter to unpack the name.

Pick a letter.

From the field to the screen

Every SCADA system follows the same path. A sensor measures something in the field. A PLC or RTU turns that signal into a number. A communication link carries it away. The SCADA host shows it to the operator, and the operator's commands travel back the same way.

How it scales

A real system has one control centre and many field sites, sometimes hundreds, spread across a state or a country. They are joined by a wide-area network.

The role of SCADA in TNB

For Tenaga Nasional Berhad, SCADA gives system operators enough information and control to run the power system, or part of it, safely and securely. Typical tasks:

Manage the grid Separate load across busbars in dual-mode supply Power factor correction Manage power supply Manage load restrictions Metering and billing

1.4RTU vs PLC

Both can serve as the outstation. The short version: an RTU cannot really process data, it collects it and sends it to SCADA. A PLC can process the data itself.

PLCRTU
LocationBest for local-area controlRemote locations, long communication distances
SCADA linkMay or may not be connected to SCADAAlmost always connected to a SCADA master
ProgrammingNeeds external programmingOften simple interfaces
EnvironmentRelatively stable environmentsHigh environmental tolerance
PowerNormally on permanent powerBatteries and/or charging circuit (e.g. solar panel)
Main jobControlling valves and processes as well as monitoring; loss of power shuts the system down safelyMonitoring more than control; if power is lost, SCADA loses sight of the site but not local control

1.5Data acquisition and data flow

Data moves in two directions. In the monitoring direction, indications, alarms and measured values travel from the outstation up to the master station. In the control direction, commands and setpoints travel down to the field.

Below is a hot water tank at a remote plant. A local PLC keeps the water at its setpoint by itself: when the water is too cool it switches the heater on, and when it is warm enough it switches the heater off. This is the automatic part.

From the control room you supervise it: watch the temperature, start or stop the heater, and change the setpoint. The left side is what the operator sees on the HMI. The right side is what is really happening at the tank. They only agree when data arrives.

      Things to try
      1. Watch for a minute. The local PLC switches the heater on and off to hold about 60 °C with nobody touching it. Open the Trend tab to see it.
      2. Click heater H-101 on the HMI. Select Stop, then Execute. The command travels down, the PLC stops heating, and over the next minute the water cools on your screen.
      3. Switch to Engineer, click controller TIC-101 and raise the setpoint to 70 °C. The PLC slowly heats up to the new value by itself. Viewers and Operators cannot do this.
      4. Simulate a stuck contactor. The PLC can no longer switch the heater off, so the water keeps heating. Like a real tank this is slow: it takes about a minute to get dangerous, so keep an eye on the Trend tab. At 85 °C you get a warning; at 90 °C the safety thermostat TSH-101 cuts the heater on its own. Acknowledge the alarm, wait for the technician's repair, then reset the trip from the heater faceplate.
      5. Untick the communication link. Values go stale, commands time out, and a trip at the tank goes unseen until the link returns.
